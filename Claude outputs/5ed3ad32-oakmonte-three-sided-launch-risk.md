# Why Oakmonte Will Struggle If It Launches All Three Sides At Once

## The core issue

Oakmonte's structure — sellers, buyers, and creators — is a three-sided marketplace. Three-sided marketplaces don't have one cold-start problem; they have three, running concurrently, each dependent on the other two. A seller has no reason to list without buyer demand. A buyer has no reason to visit without seller inventory. A creator (via the model-booking layer) has no reason to invest time without both an audience and inventory worth showcasing. Remove any one side and the other two lose their reason to show up.

The two-phase plan — Phase 1: cold-DM seller acquisition, Phase 2: full launch — reads as sequencing, but it only sequences *one* side ahead of the other two. Buyers and creators are still introduced simultaneously with each other in Phase 2, at the exact moment sellers' patience (built up during a revenue-free Phase 1) runs out. That isn't three problems solved one at a time — it's one problem solved, then two solved at once, under time pressure created by Phase 1 itself.

## Why Phase 1 doesn't buy real sequencing advantage

- **Cold-DM sellers are the lowest-commitment kind of seller.** Agreeing to a DM ask ("join the waitlist," "list a few items") costs a seller almost nothing. It's not evidence of demand for the platform — it's evidence that saying yes to a DM is free. The real test — will a seller keep inventory listed, respond to buyer questions, fulfil orders — only happens once buyers show up, which is Phase 2, not Phase 1.
- **A waiting seller is a decaying asset.** Every week between sign-up and the first real sale is a week a seller can lose interest, get discouraged, or simply forget the platform exists. Phase 1's success metric (sellers gathered) has no built-in mechanism to convert into Phase 2 retention.
- **There's no intermediate proof point before the third side gets added.** A version of this phasing that actually sequences risk would look like: gather sellers, launch to buyers only, get to real transaction volume, *then* bring in creators once there's something worth promoting. Oakmonte's plan skips that step — buyers and creators both arrive in the same phase, with nothing proven in between.

## What actually happens at "full launch"

At the moment Phase 2 goes live, three things are true at once:

1. Buyers land on a marketplace with only whatever inventory cold-DM'd sellers bothered to list — thin, unvetted, unproven selection, close to worst-case for a fashion buyer's first impression.
2. Creators are being asked to invest content and reputation promoting a platform with no transaction history and no proof buyers convert — a hard ask, since audience trust is the one resource a creator can't get back.
3. Phase 1 sellers are watching in real time whether buyers materialize. If they don't — because buyer acquisition and creator-driven traffic are both unproven at the same moment — sellers churn, which makes the buyer experience worse, which discourages creators further.

That's a single, simultaneous failure mode touching all three sides together, not three independent launches spaced apart. The two-phase structure delays *when* this happens; it doesn't change *whether* it happens.

## Why this is especially unforgiving in fashion e-commerce

Fashion buyers carry high trust and selection expectations even by marketplace standards — they're judging fit, quality, and delivery reliability on inventory they can't touch. A thin, cold-start catalog reads as a low-trust environment before a single transaction occurs, which is a problem the escrow feature can't fix, since escrow protects *payment*, not *selection*. Meanwhile, "Content to Cart" and model-booking are direct-competing concepts with TikTok Shop and LTK — platforms that already have the audience scale and algorithmic distribution to make content-driven commerce work at zero marginal cost. Oakmonte is attempting the hardest version of a hard model (three-sided cold start) in the same category as incumbents who solved distribution years ago.

## Compounding structural risk

- **No liquidity existed on any side going into launch.** Phase 1 was meant to fix exactly one-third of that gap; the other two-thirds were always going to be addressed live, in public, during Phase 2.
- **Team capacity is reduced right at the point of highest execution demand.** Running seller outreach, creator outreach, and buyer acquisition simultaneously — while finishing technical work — takes more hands, not fewer, and the founding team lost a member days before launch.
- **The business isn't a registered entity.** This doesn't touch the marketplace mechanics directly, but it removes the option to raise a bridge round or bring in help quickly if Phase 2 stalls, since there's no clean legal structure to transact through.

## The sequencing that tends to actually work

Comparable multi-sided marketplaces that survived their cold start generally proved one full two-sided loop before adding a third side — LTK built a content-and-audience business first and layered commerce on afterward; Depop proved seller-buyer liquidity before creators became central to its growth. The common thread: don't introduce a new side until the existing sides are already generating real, repeatable transactions without it. Oakmonte's plan introduces two new sides in the same moment, on top of a first side that was never proven to convert.

## Bottom line

The two-phase structure creates the appearance of sequencing without the substance of it. Only one of three sides gets a head start, and that head start is measured in low-commitment sign-ups rather than proven engagement. When Phase 2 arrives, Oakmonte isn't launching the easier second act of a staged rollout — it's attempting the full three-sided cold start in one motion, with a thinner team, no revenue cushion, and inventory that was never buyer-tested. The phasing delays the hard problem by a few weeks; it doesn't make the problem smaller.
